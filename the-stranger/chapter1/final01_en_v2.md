# Reading *The Stranger* (I): "My Mother Died. It Wasn't My Fault."

*A reading of Camus's The Stranger, Chapter 1 — and why Meursault's honesty is so unsettling.*

---

"Today, my mother died. Or maybe yesterday; I don't know."

"I asked my boss for two days off. He couldn't refuse for a reason like that, but he didn't seem pleased."

"I told him, 'It wasn't my fault.' He didn't respond."

These are the opening lines of Camus's *The Stranger* — some of the most famous in all of literature.

I genuinely couldn't understand what kind of person Meursault was. His mother had just died. And the first thing he says to his annoyed boss is: "It wasn't my fault"?

---

## The Funeral

Then came the vigil:

"I drank some coffee. I felt like smoking. But I wasn't sure if I could, not in front of Mother. I thought it over. It didn't seem to matter. I offered the caretaker a cigarette and we smoked."

"At one point I had the absurd impression that they were there to judge me."

During the funeral procession:

"The sun was starting to bear down on the earth, and it was getting hot incredibly fast."

"The glare from the sky was making the whole landscape shimmer with heat — merciless and exhausting."

And at the end:

"As we entered the outskirts of Algiers, I thought about going to bed and sleeping for twelve hours straight, and I felt a rush of pleasure at the idea."

His mother dies — he doesn't cry.

At the vigil — he drinks coffee and smokes.

During the funeral — he notices the sun, the heat, his exhaustion.

After the burial — all he thinks about is going to sleep.

His every reaction seemed out of step with the grief we expected him to feel.

---

## The Outsider

So how do we understand Meursault? I found a clue while thinking about video games.

Imagine you're playing a story-driven game.

A major character dies. The music becomes heavy. Everyone around you is devastated. You know this is supposed to be a heartbreaking moment. You know the character is supposed to be grieving.

But sitting in front of the screen, you don't actually feel that much.

You think:

*Okay. When does this scene end?*

You start noticing completely irrelevant things:

What's the next quest?

I'm a bit tired.

I want to finish this chapter and take a break.

It's strange. It's not that you don't understand what happened. You understand perfectly well that this is a "big deal." It's just that the weight of it stays where the game put it — in the story — and never quite enters your body.

The game is telling you: *This is tragedy.*

But your body is telling you: *I'm tired. Let's move on.*

I suddenly realized that Meursault at the funeral might be experiencing something similar.

---

## The Source of the Disconnect

But if it's just "emotions didn't keep up," that still doesn't quite explain Meursault. Because most of us, even if we don't feel devastated in the moment, would still *know*: "This is my mother."

And that thought would pull everything else along with it:

She raised me. She was there for me. She's my mother. She's gone. So I *should* be sad.

But Meursault doesn't seem to process it that way. He doesn't keep asking:

What does my mother mean to me?

What does losing her mean?

What happens to me now?

He just keeps feeling whatever is right in front of him:

It's hot.

I'm tired.

I want a cigarette.

I want coffee.

I want to go to sleep.

So I started to think: the real disconnect isn't the absence of emotion.

**It's the absence of meaning-making.**

---

## The Absence of Meaning-Making

We all live in a world saturated with meaning.

A diploma isn't just a piece of paper. It means:

*I finished my education. I'm entering the next stage of my life.*

A ring isn't just jewelry. It means:

*We love each other. We're married.*

Even a funeral isn't just "someone died." It means:

*This was my loved one. I've lost someone important. I should grieve. I should say goodbye.*

**Meaning is like an invisible filter that turns a fact into something that matters *to me*.**

And Meursault seems to rarely reach for this filter.

His mother is dead — so his mother is dead.

The funeral is a funeral.

The sun is the sun.

Heat is heat.

Tiredness is tiredness.

He rarely connects these things into a larger narrative. That's why he seems so cold.

But here's what's interesting: **it's not that he doesn't feel anything. It's that his feelings are rarely pulled along by meaning.**

When others see a mother's death, the first thing that comes to mind is "loss."

When Meursault sees it, the first thing he *feels* is the sun, the sweat, the fatigue.

Others live in the question: **"What does this mean?"**

Meursault lives closer to: **"What does this feel like right now?"**

He feels deeply, but he rarely turns what he feels into a larger meaning.

---

## "It's Not My Fault"

This made me rethink that line: "It's not my fault."

At first, it seemed shockingly cold. Your mother just died — why is your first concern what your boss thinks of you?

But the more I thought about it, the more it fit Meursault's way of being.

He asked for time off because his mother died. His boss was unhappy. So he said:

"It's not my fault."

He didn't package the moment into a grief story. He didn't try to prove to his boss: "I really loved my mother."

He just stated a fact: my mother died, I needed time off. You're not happy about it. But that's not my fault.

The line sounds cold — even absurd. But it's also **completely honest**.

---

## The Absurd

And this is when I started thinking about the absurd.

Maybe Meursault isn't simply a strange or emotionless man.

What makes him unsettling is that he seems to be missing the system of meaning that most of us rely on to make sense of our lives.

We function normally because we constantly assign meaning to the world.

Why go to work today? To earn money.

Why earn money? To live.

Why live? Because I have family, dreams, a future.

An ordinary life chains together like this:

*Today → Tomorrow → Future → Goals → Meaning.*

We rarely stop to ask: **"Are these meanings real?"**

Because if you keep pushing, you might discover: they're not answers built into the world. They're things we've added ourselves.

And that's exactly where Camus's concept of the absurd begins.
